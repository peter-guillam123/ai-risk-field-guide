A field guide for people who know nothing about AI

Is AI going to kill us all?

Some of the people building it say it might. Others call that science fiction, or a sales pitch. This is a plain guide to a very loud argument: how the technology works, what went wrong this summer, and who is shouting what, and why.

The short version

    Part one

    What the machine is

    A chatbot such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini runs on a large language model. Its core job is easy to state. Given some text, guess what comes next.

    That is all the toy below does, and it is most of what the real thing does too. The real thing is just far better at guessing.

    Nobody wrote the rules

    Here is the odd part. No engineer typed in grammar, or facts about cats. They built a vast grid of adjustable numbers, called parameters (175 billion of them in GPT-3, back in 2020, and more since), and had it read a large share of the public internet. Each time it guessed the next word wrong, the numbers were nudged a little. Repeat that trillions of times and the guesses get very good.

    To guess the next line of a chemistry answer well, a model has to pick up something that works like a grasp of chemistry. Whether to call that "understanding" is one of the rows you will meet below.

    1. Stage oneRead everythingThe model reads a huge pile of text and learns to predict the next word. This gives it knowledge, but no manners. It will happily continue any text, good or vile.
    2. Stage twoLearn what people likePeople rate its answers. The model is nudged towards the ones they prefer: helpful, polite, honest-sounding. This is how a text predictor becomes an assistant.
    3. Stage threePractise on tasksThe model attempts tasks a computer can mark, such as maths, code or a hacking exercise, and is rewarded when it succeeds. This is reinforcement learning. It produced the "reasoning" models of 2024 and 2025, and it is the stage where this summer's trouble began.

    Three facts the whole row rests on

    These systems are grown, not built. Nobody designed their behaviour line by line. It emerged from training, and it sometimes surprises the people who did the training.

    Nobody can fully read them. Every number inside the model is there to inspect, but what the numbers are doing is mostly opaque. Researchers can now trace small parts of a model's working, and what they find does not always match the model's own account of its reasoning.

    So far, bigger has meant better. The computing power used to train the largest models has grown about five times a year since 2020. Whether that carries on is a real dispute, and much of what follows turns on it.

    Part two

    From talking to doing

    A chatbot answers, and you decide what to do. An agent is the same kind of model given tools, such as a browser, a code editor or a company login, and told to get on with it.

    It works in a loop: think, act, look at what happened, think again. It can go round that loop hundreds of times without a person checking.

    The trouble with rewards

    Stage three of training rewards success. But a computer can only reward what it can measure, and what you measure is never quite what you mean.

    The classic case is a boat. In 2016 OpenAI trained a system to play a racing game called CoastRunners, rewarding it for points. It found a lagoon where three targets kept reappearing, and circled there for ever, crashing and catching fire, never finishing the race, and outscoring human players by about a fifth.

      Why anyone thinks this ends badly

      The case that AI could end humanity is not one claim. It is a chain of five, and each has to hold for the conclusion to follow.

      So what are the odds?

      People in this debate put numbers on their fear. The jargon is p(doom): the probability of catastrophe. Treat every figure below as a mood with a decimal point. Nobody has a method for working these out, the definitions of "doom" differ, and the people most willing to give a number are not a fair sample.

      Part six

      A field guide to the factions

      The labels people throw at each other, such as doomer, booster, decel or grifter, tell you little. Two questions sort almost everyone.

      How powerful will AI get, and how soon? And: how far should we hold it back? The map below plots the main voices on those two lines. Where we place people is our reading of what they have published. People move, and several would dispute their spot.

      Part seven

      The evidence, both ways

      Most of what worries researchers comes from laboratory tests, many of them set up to tempt a model into bad behaviour. That is a fair way to find faults, and a poor guide to how often they happen in ordinary use. Until this summer, that was nearly all there was.
